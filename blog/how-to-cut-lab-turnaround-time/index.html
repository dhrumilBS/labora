Turnaround time

How to cut turnaround time in a diagnostic lab

A practical guide to measuring TAT the same way every time, finding where the hours really go, and catching delays before the doctor calls.

Key takeaways

  • Define one start and stop point for TAT, and split it into pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical time.
  • Set a target per test and priority, not one number for the whole lab.
  • Timestamp every handoff with a barcode scan so you can see where time is lost.
  • Alert the team when a case is at risk, not after it is already late.
  • Review on-time rate and delay reasons weekly, by test and by center.

Why turnaround time matters

For a patient, turnaround time is how long they wait to hear what is wrong. For a referring doctor, it decides whether a treatment starts today or tomorrow. For your lab, it is one of the few numbers both groups notice without being told, and one of the first reasons a doctor sends samples somewhere else.

The good news: most delays are not in the analyzer. They hide in the handoffs between the front desk, phlebotomy, the bench, review, and delivery. That makes turnaround one of the most fixable problems in a diagnostic lab, as long as you can see it.

Measure TAT the same way every time

If the front desk measures from registration and the lab measures from receipt, you will argue about numbers instead of fixing delays. Pick one definition, write it down, and measure every case against it.

A common starting point is registration to signed report, split into three phases so you can see which one is slipping:

PhaseStartsEndsTypical delays
Pre-analyticalPatient registeredSample received in labCollection queues, home visits, transport, labeling errors
AnalyticalSample receivedResult enteredBatching, reruns, instrument downtime, missing reagents
Post-analyticalResult enteredReport signed and sentWaiting for review, manual typing, sign-off, delivery
Tip: count calendar time, not working hoursPatients and doctors experience the clock, not your shift schedule. Report calendar time, and track working-hours time separately if you need it for staffing.

Set targets by test, not one number

A single lab-wide target hides the problem. A routine lipid profile, an urgent troponin, and an ultrasound report have very different expectations, so give each test, or test group, its own target, with a separate target for urgent priority.

  • Start from what doctors expect today. Ask your top referrers what "on time" means for the tests they order most.
  • Separate routine from urgent. Urgent cases should carry their own, shorter target and move to the front of every queue.
  • Make targets visible. A target the bench cannot see on every case is a target nobody is working toward.

Find where the hours actually go

You cannot fix what you cannot see. The fastest way to see it is to timestamp every handoff: registration, collection, receipt in the lab, rack placement, result entry, review, signature, and delivery. A barcode scan at each step does this automatically, without asking anyone to write down times.

Once the timestamps exist, sort cases by the longest phase. Patterns appear quickly: one collection center that batches transport twice a day, one test that waits for a weekly run, one reviewer who signs everything in the evening.

Fix the handoffs

With the delays visible, most fixes are operational, not technical. Work through the phases in order:

  1. Label at the point of collection. Print the barcode when the sample is drawn, so tubes never arrive unlabeled or mismatched.
  2. Move transport to a schedule. Fixed pickup times from each collection point beat "when the rider is free."
  3. Give every tube a home. Scanning a sample into a rack slot ends the search through refrigerators when a doctor calls.
  4. Record why samples are held or rejected. A reason on every rejection turns a recurring problem into something you can fix at the source.
The analyzer is rarely the bottleneck. The wait is almost always in the handoff between two people.

Catch delays before they happen

A report that is already late can only be apologized for. A report that is at risk can still be saved. Set an alert when a case reaches a share of its target, for example three quarters of the way, and send it to the person who can act: the bench lead for analytical delays, the reviewer for sign-off delays.

Keep the at-risk list short and visible on a shared screen. The goal is a team that clears amber cases before they turn red, not a dashboard that only reports how many went late yesterday.

Make sign-off and delivery instant

Post-analytical time is often the easiest to cut, because it is mostly waiting and typing. Three changes make the biggest difference:

  • Report templates for each modality and test, so doctors complete findings instead of typing whole reports.
  • Digital signatures with clear review rules, so an approved report is released immediately.
  • Automatic delivery of the signed PDF to the patient and the referring doctor, so nobody has to call to ask whether it is ready.

Review it every week

Turnaround improves when someone owns the number. Put a 20-minute review on the calendar each week and walk through the same short checklist:

  • On-time rate overall, and for urgent cases on their own
  • The five tests with the most late cases, and the phase where they slipped
  • On-time rate by collection center and by reviewer
  • The most common delay and rejection reasons this week
  • One change to try before next week's review

Small, steady changes compound. A lab that reviews turnaround weekly, and fixes one handoff at a time, gets faster month after month, and referring doctors notice.
